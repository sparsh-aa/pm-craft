# Problem Framing, Hypotheses, Ideation, Prioritisation

## Define the true problem
Define = synthesise research into insights and needs. Expect ambiguity. Rewrite the statement many times, and go back
for more data if needed. Common failure (Apple coach-marks case): the team sees low gesture usage, *assumes* bad
tutorials and jumps to solutions. It skipped Empathise.

**Problem statement template**
- **Problem**: one sentence. The gap between the current and desired state. Who, what, where, when.
- **Background**: evidence and context. Who has tried to solve it, what is known.
- **Relevance**: why it matters. Who feels it, the financial impact, other parts of the business, society.
- **Objectives**: proposed directions and their benefits.
A good statement focuses and inspires, gives criteria to judge ideas, empowers independent decisions, and is discrete
rather than "all things to all people".

**10 tools to get the framing right**
1. Rephrase: "increase productivity" → "make your job easier" (Toyota).
2. Expose and challenge assumptions: "restaurants have menus?"
3. Chunk up: "What is this part of? What's the intention behind it?"
4. Chunk down: "What are the parts or examples of this?"
5. Multiple perspectives: customer, competitor, team.
6. Language: "In what ways might I [action] [object] [qualifier] [end result]?" Make it positive and a question.
7. Make it engaging: "wow customers" rather than "increase sales".
8. Reverse it: how would we *lose* sales? Then invert the answers.
9. Gather facts: "you never listen" → "no eye contact".
10. Problem-solve the statement itself.
**5 Whys**: car won't start → battery → alternator → belt → not replaced → maintenance schedule ignored (root cause).

**How Might We**: "[User] needs [verb need] because [compelling insight]" → "How might we …?" Write several framings
(airport security: "safe, secure and joyful" / "fast and relaxing").

### Problem framing canvas (default framing tool)
Six questions, best presented as **one slide** to leadership:
1. **True problem**: a user behaviour + its cause. "Users can't log in with email and password because they are alien
   to these authentication methods." **Conversion or retention is never the problem; it is the opportunity.**
2. **Who** faces it (a problem always belongs to a user; choose the segment first).
3. **How do we know it's real**: data that localises it + research that explains it.
4. **Value to customers**.
5. **Value to business** (sized: value ≈ users facing it × share who'd benefit or pay).
6. **Why now** (urgency, trend).
Questions 5 and 6 are the two gates before pursuing any problem (the same as the Sequoia pitch template: size + why now).
"Our job is not to address every customer opportunity. It is to address the ones that help us reach the desired business
outcome." Don't memorise the canvas; it is the natural conversation: why → where → who → how do you know → value → why now.

- **Three sources of problems**: metrics on an existing product; conversations with potential customers (zero-to-one);
  macro or industry trends.
- **Two kinds of problem statement**: "increase metric X" (no reference point) and "X is lower than a reference" (a gap
  to close). Same approach; only the opportunity sizing differs.
- **Prioritise twice**: at problem level first, then at solution level. Never rank items from different problems in
  one list.
- Founder hunches aren't validation. Validate that the problem is real and that solving it has outsized, urgent impact.
- Worked example: Flipkart mobile-web login (₹1,000 crore) in `09-worked-cases.md`.

## Hypotheses
Testable, with 5 parts: **change, expected impact, who, how much, by when**.
"Adding more search filters will lead 30% more shoppers to find a specific product within 1 minute of searching."
Experiment form: "We believe [change] will cause [outcome] because [mechanism]." The *because* is mandatory.
Decide in advance what you'll do if it passes, fails or shows no impact.

**Solution-hypothesis sentence**: "If we make *this change*, we will realise *this outcome*, because it
addresses *this customer need*." Five elements:
1. **Change** (the specific solution).
2. **Outcome**, sized through the KPI tree (+1% date selection → X% conversion → $Y).
3. **Segment** (e.g. users without dates on the hotel page, desktop only).
4. **Measurement defined before building**, including the downstream metric. If dates entered rise but conversion
   doesn't, the hypothesis is invalidated.
5. **By when**, allowing for adoption: most users aren't on the latest app version, and few discover new features
   ("the consumer cares about their problem, not your product"). Design for awareness.
Example: "Opening the calendar on page load will increase visits with dates entered, for desktop users only, raising
conversion by X%, realised N days after rollout." Measure a feature's own usage and repeat usage first, then the outcome.

**Test the riskiest hypothesis first.** One false belief can break the whole model (e.g. an edtech "people want shorter
courses" chain).

**Levels of product analysis (Torres)** — know which level a test sits at:
1. Problem exists / value proposition
2. Feature that delivers the value
3. Design of that feature
4. Technical feasibility
A failed design ≠ a failed feature ≠ no problem. On failure, move **up** a level. If search filters failed, maybe
recommendations or relevance would work better. Do shoppers even want speed, or do they enjoy browsing?

## Ideation
Principles: quantity over quality, suspend judgement, every voice heard (design for introverts), **novelty over
relevance** (relevance comes naturally; aim for novel and relevant).
Methods: brainstorm, braindump (silent post-its), brainwrite (3 minutes then pass left), brainwalk, analogies, creative
pause, crowdstorm (reviews, surveys), cheatstorm (competitors and other industries), bodystorm (act as the user),
worst possible idea, sketch/storyboard, charrette (users ideate with you), prototyping as ideation.
Facilitation: judgement-free, team win, 15–20 minutes per prompt, visible timer, "Yes, and…", a parking lot for
tangents, one idea at a time as short headlines. Converge with coloured dot votes by category (low-hanging, most
realistic, most exciting). Don't kill wild ideas early.

**Outcome → Problem → Solution**: never judge an idea without the problem it solves and the outcome it targets.

Meeting test (Alex Lowe): frame every brainstorm by the outcome ("how might we increase gross margin on this SKU") so
arguments point back to it; when someone brings an idea, ask for its outcome and problem before judging it.
**New-product sandbox** (Strategy Umwelt): look where three circles overlap — **market weakness** (friction people
hate), **internal strengths** (skills, assets, distribution) and **adjacent possibility** (what just became technically
possible). Pairwise overlaps give idea zones; the centre gives MVP hypotheses.
Example: 90% course completion (outcome) ← high drop-off in the first 7 days (problem) ← leaderboard, WhatsApp
reminders, progress visibility, rewards (solutions).

**Mind-map method: Goal / Because / While-Without / By.** State the goal, why it matters, constraints (things to do or
avoid) and solutions. Attach "because I assume…" or "because I know…" to each node, so assumptions are explicit and
testable. Each solution becomes a sub-problem with its own Because/While/By. Keep it on the wall and update it as
assumptions are proven or disproven. It scales from "make $70 today" to "ARR → $6M without >10% services revenue,
while keeping morale and churn <5%".
Mind map = a tree with one parent per node (expands one topic). Concept map = labelled relationships with multiple
parents (causes, systems). Cognitive map = free-form mental model, useful inside interviews.

**Ideate at three levels**: simplest (newsletters, flashcards, ask-a-mentor), stretch (alumni mentors, AI
mock interviews), **outrageous** (<1% probability but technically possible; Google Loon). Then filter with the five
risks: **value, usability, feasibility, viability, scalability**. Examples: content subscriptions failed *viability* for
an edtech platform (not a content distributor, conflicts with the affordability play); audiobook summaries failed *value* (users
wanted live over recorded); heavy animations hurt *feasibility* on real network speeds; BookMyShow's Coldplay queue
failed *scalability*. **Ideation is collective**: TripAdvisor's winning calendar idea came from a designer. "Human
nature is to fall in love with ideas — that is exactly what you have to combat."

**Mind-map a chosen solution into requirements** (built live for a mentoring platform):
1. **Actors** (mentors, mentees, the company).
2. **Benefits per actor** (addresses value risk).
3. **Constraints / do-not-wants per actor** (these become requirements; e.g. mentees don't want spam from mentors
   selling courses; the company doesn't want high payouts or legal exposure).
4. **Interactions** between actors → the **views** needed (mentor, mentee, admin).
5. **Jobs/steps per view** (register → profile → get paired → schedule → deliver → feedback → earnings → support).
6. **Detail each step** (profile fields), then wireframe.
Constraints can become features (Uber masks both parties' numbers). Actor ≠ segment (one actor has many segments).

**Backlog hierarchy**: saga (with why + opportunity size) → epics (one per view) → user stories → tasks → subtasks
(bio: open text, character limit, no junk characters). Granularity lets engineers estimate and exposes dependencies
(a mentee filter by experience needs the mentor profile to store experience).

## Generating solutions properly
- At least **3 distinct** technology-led solutions. Distinct means mutually exclusive approaches (voice vs chatbot vs
  rewards), not chatbot vs chatbot+ML. Don't propose what the product already does.
- For each: key idea, how it works, tech components, benefits.
- Map root causes first (mind map). Spotify infrequent-user example: no contextual prompts, competitors own routine
  moments, content–context mismatch, "commitment" perception, no daily triggers.

## Prioritising solutions

### Two-level method (default prioritisation answer — use this by default)
Formula frameworks used as plug-and-play (MoSCoW, RICE with arbitrary high/med/low = 8/5/2 scores) are "rice dal
khichdi frameworks": units don't balance and scores get force-fitted. Instead:
1. **Problem level first.** Roadmap = plan from current to future state. **Themes = product outcomes** (from the KPI
   tree). Rank themes by the company's priority this cycle (usually a quarter) and allocate **engineering bandwidth by
   %** (e.g. B 60% / A 30% / C 10%). This is a collaborative call between product, engineering and business; "there is
   no science behind this". The quarter before a big sale may be almost all scalability work.
2. **Capacity** = developers × weeks × **load factor** (share of time actually coding new features: typically
   **30–50%**, ~40% in a very good org; 60% is unrealistic). 10 devs × 12 weeks × 50% = 60 usable dev-weeks, then split
   by theme.
3. **Within a theme, score each solution**:
   - **Effort**: always from engineering, estimated from user stories, including QA, integration and staging. "Never
     try to be the hero and commit on behalf of engineering." Convert to a contextual 1–10 scale (<1 dev-week = 1).
   - **Impact**: KPI tree + guesstimate (playback speed: 5% open Settings × 18% pick a speed × completion lift per user
     → Δ completion). Convert to a contextual 1–10 scale (<2% lift = 1).
   - **Confidence by process stage**: idea 0.1 → one-pager 0.2 → data-validated problem 0.3 → research-validated 0.4
     → stakeholder-aligned → planned and estimated → prototype/usability-validated → experiment data (highest).
     Top-down "just build it" asks score low, which helps push back.
   - **Product score = impact × confidence − effort** (gain minus spend; scores, not raw units).
4. **Rank, fill the theme's capacity in order, spill the rest** into the next quarter.
- Startups: estimates are rough but directionally right; strategy churn breaks prioritisation; small teams take only the
  top 1–2 priorities and use T-shirt sizing. When a founder overrides you, first understand their why (they see investor
  targets), then check which theme the ask belongs to.

### Interview shorthand
RICE condensed to **impact × effort**: impact = expected lift in the target metric (% or basis points); effort in
**sprints** (1 sprint = 2 weeks) or **T-shirt sizes** (S = a few days, M = 1–2 sprints, L = 2+ sprints, assuming 1–2
developers). Add **time to market** as a solution-shaping habit: "what can I launch in a week that tests the same
hypothesis?" (a fintech tested a time-bound cashback before redesigning rewards). B2B customers hand you a feature list, so
impact × effort is enough; in B2C nobody knows what users want, so speed of learning matters more.
"Anybody can be clever and come up with solutions, but how do you prioritise? This becomes the hard part."

### Other methods (reference only)
**Risk/likelihood scoring matrix** (common in case work): score each option 1–3 on value risk, usability risk, feasibility
risk, viability risk, scalability risk, and likelihood of moving the product outcome (1 = high risk / low likelihood,
3 = low risk / high likelihood). Total out of 18, pick the highest, and **write the reasoning in each cell**.
Spotify: integrations 16 > activity-based ML recs 15 (feasibility 1, not enough data) > gamification 14.

**RICE** = Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. Also: ICE; value vs complexity (Productboard); impact/effort with a
capacity line. Evaluators notice force-fitted scores, so keep them honest. The critique above applies.

**Roadmap shape**: current state → future state; themes = product outcomes; activities grouped under themes;
cross-team dependencies flagged early (e.g. a support-reduction theme that needs the payments PM).

**Bug prioritisation** = users affected × trust risk (payments > catalogue; high-value segments get P0).

**Roadmap prioritisation under constraint** (edtech case): tie every item to the company goal (e.g. active learners
↑, cost ↓, new vertical), score impact against effort in dev-weeks, respect real capacity (e.g. 2 engineers × one
quarter). Include cheap high-leverage items (a 1-week referral leaderboard, 2-week international payments). Fund
enabling work for strategic bets (coding playground for a new vertical). Don't starve reliability (CI/CD, logs,
pre-prod reduce on-calls). State what is cut and why.
Balance quick wins vs platform investments, and offence (growth) vs defence (tech debt, bugs, ops).
